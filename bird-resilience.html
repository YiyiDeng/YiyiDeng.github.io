RESEARCH · 01 · BIODIVERSITY & CLIMATE

Climate resilience of flagship bird communities

A China–United States comparison of bird community resilience and future climate refugia under climate change.

Pioneer Research Program · Research Project

Research overview

This project investigates how climate change may reshape the resilience of flagship bird communities across China and the United States. By combining species distribution modeling, community-level resilience metrics, and future climate projections, the study moves beyond species richness to examine where bird communities may be more capable of persisting under environmental change.

Research question

How does climate change influence the spatial resilience of flagship bird communities, and where may future climate refugia occur in China and the United States?

Why this question?

Climate change is reshaping species distributions and ecological communities. However, species richness alone does not fully describe whether a community can remain resilient when environmental conditions change. A community with multiple species performing similar ecological functions may be better buffered against species loss, while differences in species' responses to environmental conditions can further influence community stability. This project therefore combines functional redundancy and response diversity to develop a spatial assessment of bird community resilience.

Methodology

Species distribution models were developed using GBIF occurrence records and environmental predictors. MaxEnt was used to estimate species-level habitat suitability under current and projected future climate conditions. The resulting species distributions were then integrated to quantify bird community resilience through functional redundancy and response diversity.

02 · Future climate refugia

The second major output builds directly on the resilience assessment. Current and future bird community resilience were compared under projected climate scenarios to identify areas where resilience may be maintained, gained, or decline in ways relevant to future biodiversity persistence.

From resilience to refugia

Future climate refugia were identified by examining changes in bird community resilience between current conditions and future climate projections. This allows the analysis to move from describing where resilience currently occurs toward identifying areas that may remain important for biodiversity under future climate change.

Stable refugia

Areas with high current resilience that remain highly resilient under future climate conditions.

Transitional refugia

Areas where resilience may increase under future climate conditions, creating potential opportunities for biodiversity persistence.

Potential refugia

Areas with current resilience that may decline under future climate change but remain relevant for conservation planning.

PIONEER RESEARCH PROGRAM

From a research question to a complete study

I conducted this research as part of the Pioneer Research Program, where I developed the project from an ecological research question into a quantitative study combining species distribution modeling, spatial analysis, and machine learning. The project gave me the opportunity to explore how computational approaches can be used to address real-world questions in biodiversity conservation under climate change.

Conservation implications

The combination of bird community resilience mapping and future climate refugia identification provides a spatial framework for climate-adaptive biodiversity conservation. Rather than focusing only on where biodiversity is currently concentrated, the results highlight the importance of considering both present resilience and potential future changes.

From resilience to conservation

In China, conservation planning may benefit from prioritizing areas approaching critical climatic thresholds and preventing further habitat degradation. In the United States, maintaining elevational and landscape connectivity may be particularly important for preserving the buffering capacity of mountainous and resilient regions. Together, these strategies highlight how conservation priorities may need to differ across geographic contexts.

This project changed the way I think about biodiversity. A map of species richness can tell us where species are, but resilience asks a different question:

What happens when the environment changes — and where might biodiversity persist?
